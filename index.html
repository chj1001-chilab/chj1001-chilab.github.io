A skeptical evaluator of clinical AI

I am a researcher in the Department of Information Medicine at Asan Medical Center, Seoul, working at the intersection of treatment-response prediction and rigorous clinical-reality validation. Over five years in a hospital research lab I have led three first-author studies, and my through-line has been moving prediction away from static patient states and toward the responses a treatment will produce.

That trajectory runs from tabular machine learning for dose decision support, through time-series deep learning of treatment response, to a controlled appraisal of large language models on electronic health records — held together by one conviction: a prediction only has value once it can be safely turned into an intervention. I do not trust a model until it has been tested against clinical reality, so I judge my own work by external validation, calibration, and net clinical benefit rather than leaderboard accuracy alone.

For my PhD I want to build safe, validated, action-oriented decision support that forecasts how patients respond to treatment and, critically, knows when to hand the decision back to a clinician. My concrete anchor is reviving a held project on personalized sepsis-bundle recommendation as goal-conditioned / offline reinforcement learning, grounded in causal estimation of intervention effects.

Current focus

  • Prediction-to-intervention modeling for actionable decision support
  • Treatment-response & trajectory-under-intervention modeling from EHR
  • Goal-conditioned & offline RL for treatment recommendation
  • Selective prediction / learning-to-defer, with calibration as a safety signal
  • Clinical-utility-grounded evaluation (decision curve analysis, external validation)
  • Cross-site generalization & privacy-preserving on-premise adaptation

Research trajectory

Three first-author studies, read as one arc — from predicting a patient's state, to forecasting their response to a treatment, to asking whether foundation models can earn the same clinical trust.

2023 Stage 01 · State Scientific Reports First author · Published

Machine learning for warfarin discharge dosing

Tabular ML · decision support for the initial dose

Warfarin has a narrow therapeutic window and pharmacogenetic testing is slow and costly, so clinicians must set an initial dose from limited early data. I trained models on 17 routine EMR features collected within two days of admission — no genetic data — to suggest a personalized dose, interpreted them with SHAP, and benchmarked the predictions against five physicians and against prior pharmacogenetic algorithms.

  • MAE 0.9 mg vs 1.3 mg (physicians)
  • External validation · MIMIC-III n=891
  • Reader study · 5 physicians (ICC 0.36)
  • XGBoost · ANN · RF · LR

Validating against clinical judgment — not just a held-out test set — is what turns a regression model into a decision-support claim.

Paper ↗ Sci Rep 13:22461
2024 Stage 02 · Response Scientific Reports First author · Published

Forecasting weight change for diuretic titration

Time-series deep learning · treatment-response forecasting

Loop-diuretic dosing in heart failure lacks robust guidelines, so I built TSFD-LSTM — a bidirectional LSTM seq2seq model with a dot-product attention mechanism — to forecast a patient's 48-hour weight change after intravenous diuretic injection from 65-variable multivariate EHR sequences. An attention ablation and a Transformer baseline sharpened the comparison, and attention heatmaps exposed which features and time steps drove each forecast.

  • Accuracy within 1 kg 85.3%
  • MAE 0.56 · RMSE 1.20
  • Beats Transformer (58.9%) & RF (61.2%)
  • 4,720 patients · 182,386 rows

This was the pivot: predicting the trajectory an intervention produces is a fundamentally different — and more useful — problem than classifying a patient's state.

Paper ↗ Sci Rep 14:17723
2026 Stage 03 · Foundation Manuscript First author · Under review

Large language models vs. traditional ML for tabular clinical prediction

LLM foundation models · does capability replace hand-crafted pipelines?

Tree-based ML dominates tabular clinical prediction but depends on task-specific preprocessing that loses information. I fine-tuned open-weight LLMs (Qwen2.5 3–14B, Llama3.1-8B) with LoRA/PEFT on a single GPU — under a strict "data never leaves the hospital" constraint — feeding EHR records serialized verbatim as key–value text, then ran a same-data, same-split comparison against XGBoost and CatBoost across three tasks, judged by discrimination, calibration, and decision curve analysis.

  • MACE AUROC 0.81 ≈ XGBoost 0.80
  • Calibration lags (ECE ↑)
  • Positive net benefit (DCA)
  • 3 tasks · verbatim serialization

Capability converges, but trust does not: the same clinical yardstick I apply to any model must apply to the hyped ones too. LLMs are a chapter of this story — not the destination.
